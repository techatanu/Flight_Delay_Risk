\begin{abstract}
Phase~1 of this project predicted, on the evening before departure, whether a flight
leaving New York in 2013 will arrive 15 or more minutes late, using logistic regression
and XGBoost on 35 leakage-free schedule, congestion and delay-history features. In
Phase~2 we keep the same features, decision time, chronological split and metrics, and
ask whether deep learning adds anything. We train, in PyTorch, (i)~a compact MLP with
learned embeddings for carrier, origin and destination, (ii)~a temporal model that adds
a GRU reading the 14 days of airport, carrier and system delay statistics before the
flight, and (iii)~two hybrids with the Phase~1 XGBoost: tree-leaf embeddings fed into
the network, and a probability blend. The temporal model, selected on validation,
reaches a December PR-AUC of \TemporalTestPR{} against \XgboostTestPR{} for XGBoost
(day-level bootstrap 95\% interval of the difference
$[\TemporalCILow,\,\TemporalCIHigh]$) and raises the best achievable recall at 50\%
precision from \XgboostRecallPfifty{} to \TemporalRecallPfifty. Neither hybrid beats
the temporal network. Ablations over three seeds show that the GRU recovers most of the
signal when the hand-made history features are removed, that early stopping is the
regulariser that matters, and that the learned embeddings bring no measurable gain over
one-hot inputs.
\end{abstract}

flight delay, deep learning, entity embeddings, GRU, hybrid models, calibration

\section{Introduction}
Delays cost U.S. airlines and passengers billions of dollars a year, and a risk score
available the evening before a flight lets an airline warn passengers, re-plan crews or
consider moving a departure \cite{sternberg2017review}. Phase~1 built such a score from
features known at that decision time: the published schedule, scheduled congestion and
smoothed delay rates of carriers, airports, routes and aircraft over strictly earlier
days. XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost} reached a December PR-AUC of \XgboostTestPR{} and
logistic regression \LrTestPR, against a base rate of \PriorRateTestPR.

The project brief names the gap these models leave: manual route and congestion
summaries ``may miss complex interactions between time, airport, carrier and recent
delay dynamics''. A seven-day mean and yesterday's rate compress a trajectory into two
numbers; a storm that builds over three days and one that hit a week ago can look the
same. Phase~2 therefore asks three questions, all under the Phase~1 protocol so that
the comparison is fair:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Does a compact network with learned category embeddings, the model required by
  the brief, improve on the Phase~1 models?
\item Does reading the ordered daily history with a recurrent layer add information
  beyond the hand-made summaries?
\item Does combining the trees with the network (a hybrid) improve on both?
\end{enumerate}
Our contributions are a leakage-tested sequence input built only from earlier days,
a PyTorch embedding MLP and temporal GRU model tuned on validation only, two hybrids
with an explicit rationale, an ablation study over eleven variants and three seeds, and
a calibration and operating-point analysis under the strong base-rate shift into
December.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Deep learning for flight delays}
Kim et al.~\cite{kim2016deep} used recurrent networks on sequences of daily airport
delay states to predict day-level delay, followed by a second network for individual
flights, and reported gains over shallow models. Gui et al.~\cite{gui2020} compared an
LSTM and random forests on large aviation data and found the LSTM prone to overfitting,
with the forest more accurate on their task. Yazdi et al.~\cite{yazdi2020} combined a
stacked denoising autoencoder with Levenberg--Marquardt training. Rebollo and
Balakrishnan~\cite{rebollo2014} and Pyrgiotis et al.~\cite{pyrgiotis2013} showed that
network-wide delay states and their propagation carry much of the predictive signal.
This motivates our temporal branch: it reads exactly such daily delay states, but only
of days before the flight. Most published studies use random splits and features
recorded after departure, which inflates results~\cite{kaufman2012leakage}; we keep the
Phase~1 decision time and chronological split.

\subsection{Deep learning on tabular data}
Benchmarks consistently find that tuned tree ensembles match or beat neural networks on
medium-sized tabular data \cite{grinsztajn2022,shwartz2022}, and that a well-regularised
MLP is a strong neural baseline \cite{gorishniy2021}. We therefore do not assume that the
network will win, and compare against the tuned Phase~1 XGBoost rather than a weak tree.
Entity embeddings \cite{guo2016entity} map each category to a learned dense vector so
that similar carriers or airports can share statistical strength; we size them with the
fast.ai rule \cite{howard2020fastai}. For sequences we use a GRU
\cite{cho2014gru}, a gated recurrent unit with fewer parameters than an LSTM
\cite{hochreiter1997}, which suits our short 14-step inputs.

\subsection{Hybrids, regularisation and calibration}
He et al.~\cite{he2014facebook} used the leaves of boosted trees as input features of a
linear model, and DeepGBM \cite{ke2019deepgbm} distils tree structure into a network;
Wide~\&~Deep \cite{cheng2016wide} joins a memorising component and a generalising one.
Our leaf hybrid follows these ideas. Against overfitting we use dropout
\cite{srivastava2014dropout}, decoupled weight decay (AdamW)
\cite{kingma2015adam,loshchilov2019adamw} and early stopping \cite{prechelt1998}.
Modern networks are often over-confident; Guo et al.~\cite{guo2017calibration} propose
temperature scaling, which we compare with Platt scaling \cite{platt1999} and isotonic
regression \cite{zadrozny2002}.

\section{Data and Decision-Time Protocol}
We use the 2013 NYC flights data \cite{nycflights13}, a public extract of the BTS
on-time database \cite{bts_ontime}: 336{,}776 departures from EWR, JFK and LGA, of
which 327{,}346 have an arrival delay. The target is $y=\mathbf{1}[\text{arrival delay}
\ge 15\,\text{min}]$. Cancelled and diverted flights have no label; they are not used as
examples but still count in the scheduled-volume features.

\textbf{Decision time and split.} Predictions are made the evening before the flight.
All 35 inputs are the Phase~1 features: 3 categorical (carrier, origin, destination) and
32 numeric (cyclic times, scheduled congestion, 15 historical delay rates over strictly
earlier dates). The split is unchanged: train January--September (244{,}737 flights,
25.0\% delayed), validation October--November (55{,}589, 17.8\%), test December (27{,}020,
33.5\%). Every hyperparameter, epoch count, calibration method, window, hybrid weight
and the choice of the final model is made on validation. December is only scored.

\textbf{Network inputs.} Numeric features are median-imputed and standardised with
training statistics. Categories are mapped to integer ids from the training vocabulary,
with id~0 for categories never seen in training (one destination).

\textbf{Daily history sequence.} For each calendar day we compute ten values: the system,
origin-airport and carrier delay rates, the mean arrival delay and cancellation share of
the system and the origin, the origin's number of departures, and a validity flag. Carrier
rates are shrunk towards the system rate with 10 pseudo-flights, because small carriers
fly few legs a day. For a flight on day $d$ the GRU reads days $d-14,\dots,d-1$ in time
order for its origin and carrier; days before 1 January are zero-padded with the flag set
to~0. Fig.~\ref{fig:daily} shows the origin delay rates: December is clearly the worst
month, the shift Phase~1 already found.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{daily_origin_rate.pdf}
\caption{Daily share of delayed arrivals per origin airport (7-day mean). December, the
test month (grey), is far above the validation months.}
\label{fig:daily}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Leakage test for the sequences.} As in Phase~1, we overwrite every outcome from
10~February onwards with random values, rebuild the daily table and check that the
28-day sequences of all 33{,}936 flights up to that date are bit-identical. A
deliberately leaky version, in which the sequence ends on the flight day, fails the
test, so the test can detect leakage.

\subsection{Embedding MLP}
For a flight with standardised numeric features $x\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$ and categories
$(c,o,d)$, the input of the MLP is
\begin{equation}
u = [\,x;\ E_c[c];\ E_o[o];\ E_d[d]\,],
\end{equation}
where $E_c\in\mathbb{R}^{17\times 8}$, $E_o\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 3}$ and
$E_d\in\mathbb{R}^{104\times 10}$ are learned tables (one extra row each for the unknown
id, fixed at zero). The size $\min(10, \mathrm{round}(1.6\,n^{0.56}))$ for $n$ categories
\cite{howard2020fastai} gives 8, 3 and 10. An embedding lookup is equivalent to a linear
layer applied to a one-hot vector, but it is cheaper and lets the gradient update only the
rows that occur in a batch. Two hidden layers follow:
\begin{align}
a_1 &= \mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(W_1 u + b_1)),\\
a_2 &= \mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(W_2 a_1 + b_2)),\\
\hat p &= \sigma(w_3^{\top} a_2 + b_3).
\end{align}
The output bias is initialised to $\mathrm{logit}(\bar y_{\text{train}})$ so that training
starts from the base rate rather than from $\hat p = 0.5$.

\subsection{Temporal branch}
The 14 daily vectors $s_1,\dots,s_{14}$ enter a GRU \cite{cho2014gru}:
\begin{align}
z_t &= \sigma(W_z s_t + U_z h_{t-1}),\quad r_t = \sigma(W_r s_t + U_r h_{t-1}),\\
\tilde h_t &= \tanh(W_h s_t + U_h(r_t \odot h_{t-1})),\\
h_t &= (1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde h_t .
\end{align}
The update gate $z_t$ decides how much of the running summary to replace; the reset gate
$r_t$ lets the unit forget old days when a new pattern starts. The last state
$h_{14}\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$ is concatenated to $u$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Because the gates
are learned, the GRU can weight recent days more or less than the fixed 7-day and
previous-day windows of the Phase~1 features.

\subsection{Hybrid models}
\textbf{Leaf hybrid (trees $\rightarrow$ network).} The Phase~1 XGBoost (308 trees of
depth~4, trained on January--September) splits the numeric history features at sharp
thresholds, which a ReLU network approximates only with many units. Each tree sends a
flight to one leaf, so a flight is described by 308 leaf ids. Following
\cite{he2014facebook,ke2019deepgbm}, every leaf gets a learned vector of size
\LeafDim; the mean of a flight's 308 vectors (an \texttt{EmbeddingBag}) is concatenated
to the temporal model's input. The trees supply the partitions, the network learns how
to weigh them together with the embeddings and the GRU. The leaf vector size, learning
rate and a dropout on the leaf vector were searched on validation
(selected: size \LeafDim, learning rate \LeafLR, dropout \LeafDropout).

\textbf{Blend (late fusion).} $\hat p = w\,\hat p_{\text{temporal}} +
(1-w)\,\hat p_{\text{XGB}}$ with $w\in\{0,0.1,\dots,1\}$ chosen on validation log loss
(selected $w=\BlendWeight$). It can only help if the two models make different errors.

\section{Training and Regularisation}
All networks minimise the binary cross-entropy
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\big[y_i\log\hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\big],
\end{equation}
computed on the logit for numerical stability. Its gradient with respect to the logit is
simply $\hat p_i - y_i$. We do not weight the classes: Phase~1 showed that weighting
leaves the ranking unchanged but pushes every probability up, and calibrated
probabilities are a required output. The optimiser is AdamW
\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with batch size 1024, decoupled weight decay on weights and
embeddings (not on biases), and gradient-norm clipping at~5 to protect the GRU.

The main danger is overfitting to individual training days. Several inputs, such as
yesterday's system delay rate, are constant within a day, so together they identify the
date, and a flexible model can memorise which training days were bad. Phase~1 met the
same problem with XGBoost and needed leaves of at least 2{,}000 hessian weight. For the
networks we use dropout \cite{srivastava2014dropout}, weight decay and, above all, early
stopping \cite{prechelt1998}: after every epoch we score validation PR-AUC, stop after
four epochs without improvement and restore the best epoch.

\textbf{Hyperparameter search.} The grid crosses hidden sizes $\{(64,32),(128,64)\}$,
dropout $\{0.1,0.3\}$, weight decay $\{10^{-4},10^{-2}\}$ and learning rate
$\{10^{-3},3\cdot10^{-4}\}$; the temporal model also tries GRU sizes 16 and 32
(16 settings for the MLP, 32 for the temporal model, seed 42). Both models selected
$(64,32)$ units, dropout 0.3, weight decay $10^{-4}$ and learning rate $10^{-3}$, and a
16-unit GRU. The validation PR-AUC varies by less than 0.01 over the whole grid, so the
result does not hinge on a lucky setting. The selected models were retrained with seeds
42, 43 and 44; seed~42 was fixed in advance as the reported model.

\textbf{Sanity checks.} With dropout and decay switched off, the temporal network drives
the training loss on 256 flights below 0.05, so model and training loop can fit data.
Reloading every saved model reproduces its predictions exactly.

Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} shows the behaviour that motivates early stopping: training loss
keeps falling while validation loss turns up after a few epochs. The best epoch is
\MLPEpoch{} for the MLP and \TemporalEpoch{} for the temporal model.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
We report PR-AUC (average precision) as the headline metric for the imbalanced target
\cite{saito2015}, ROC-AUC, the Brier score, log loss and the expected calibration error
(ECE, 10 equal-width bins). The operating point follows Phase~1: the lowest threshold
with validation precision $\geq 0.5$ is fixed and applied to December. We also report
the best recall reachable at precision $\geq 0.5$ on the December curve itself, which is
only a reference for what a threshold could achieve.

\textbf{Calibration.} Temperature scaling $\hat p=\sigma(z/T)$ \cite{guo2017calibration},
Platt scaling $\hat p=\sigma(az+b)$ and isotonic regression are each fitted on October and
scored on November and vice versa; the method with the lower mean Brier score is refitted
on all of validation. As in Phase~1 we also apply a rolling Platt recalibration that is
refitted every day on the previous $w$ days of out-of-sample scores and outcomes (known at
the decision time), with $w\in\{7,14,28\}$ chosen on November.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} Flights on the same day share weather and are strongly correlated,
so resampling single flights would give intervals that are far too narrow. We resample
the 31 December days with replacement (2{,}000 replicates) and compute the PR-AUC
difference of two models on the same resampled days, a block bootstrap with one-day
blocks \cite{kunsch1989}. Neural-network results are also repeated over three seeds.

\subsection{Hybrids}
Both hybrids improve on XGBoost (leaf hybrid \LeafHybridTestPR, blend
\BlendTestPR; bootstrap intervals above zero in Table~\ref{tab:boot}), so the network
adds value to the tree model. Neither, however, improves on the temporal network: the
leaf hybrid is slightly below it on validation (\LeafHybridValPR{} vs.\ \TemporalValPR)
and on December, and the interval of their difference contains zero. Its search was
informative. Without dropout on the leaf vector it reached its best validation score in
the first epoch: the roughly 40{,}000 extra parameters (one vector per leaf id; \HybridParams{}
in total) let the network memorise training days faster. Dropout of 0.5 on the averaged leaf vector was
needed to make it competitive. The blend puts only \BlendWeight{} of the weight on the
network because that lowers the validation log loss, but it lowers the PR-AUC: the two models'
validation scores have a correlation of 0.83 and XGBoost is the weaker one, so averaging
adds little independent information. In this problem the partitions the trees learn are
built from the same 35 features the network already sees; a hybrid would need a signal
the network lacks (for example weather forecasts) to add value beyond it.

\textbf{History is the core signal, and the GRU can learn it.} Removing the 15 Phase~1
history features and the GRU drops December PR-AUC to \AblNoHistNoGRUTest. With the GRU
reading the raw 14-day sequence instead, it recovers to \AblNoHistTest: the recurrent
branch learns from the ordered daily states most of what the hand-made rates encode. With
both, the extra gain of the GRU is small (\AblBaseTest{} vs.\ \AblNoGRUTest{} without
it). This variant also shows why validation must not be read in isolation: without any
history the model scores \emph{best} on validation (\AblNoHistNoGRUVal) yet worst on
December. October and November were calm months in which yesterday's delays say little,
whereas December was driven by multi-day weather episodes.

\textbf{Sequence length.} Seven days performs slightly better on December
(\AblSevenDaysTest) than 14 or 28 days, within one to two seed standard deviations:
recent days matter most, older days add noise.

\textbf{Embeddings bring no measurable gain.} One-hot categories (\AblOneHotTest) and even
no category input at all (\AblNoCatTest) are at least as good as learned embeddings on
December. The carrier, airport and route histories already describe the categories, so
their identities add little, and the embeddings slightly overfit category-specific
patterns that change in December. This is consistent with tabular benchmarks
\cite{grinsztajn2022} and is an honest negative result for the component the brief
emphasises.

\textbf{Regularisation.} Removing dropout or weight decay changes PR-AUC by less than the
seed spread, but training a fixed 40 epochs without early stopping lowers PR-AUC from
\AblBaseVal{} to \AblNoESVal{} on validation and from \AblBaseTest{} to \AblNoESTest{} on
December (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}). For these small networks, early stopping is the
regulariser that matters.

\subsection{Calibration}
Table~\ref{tab:cal} and Fig.~\ref{fig:cal} show the base-rate shift Phase~1 already
found. The raw network outputs are reasonably calibrated on December (ECE
\TemporalRawECE), because the network keeps roughly the training base rate of 25\%,
which is closer to December's 33.5\% than the 17.8\% of the calibration months. Calibrating on October--November, where only 17.8\% of
flights were delayed, teaches the model that delays are rare and makes December
\emph{worse} (ECE \TemporalCalECE). The Oct/Nov cross-check selected Platt scaling for
every network; temperature scaling, the usual fix for over-confident networks
\cite{guo2017calibration}, cannot move the base rate and was not chosen. The rolling
Platt recalibration over the previous \FinalRollWindow{} days adapts to the shift
(ECE \TemporalRollECE, as for XGBoost: \XgboostRollECE). Calibration under drift is a
property of the deployment procedure more than of the model \cite{gama2014}.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The networks improve discrimination at the decision time the project prescribes, and the
improvement is concentrated where it is useful: flights with the clearest risk. The
largest single effect, however, comes from history, not from architecture: whichever model
reads the past (as features or as a sequence) does well, and models that do not fail in
December. The value of the GRU is that it learns this from raw daily states.

The limits are those of Phase~1 plus some of our own. The data cover one year and three
airports, so December is a single test month and the test set has only 31 independent
days; the bootstrap intervals are correspondingly wide. No weather forecasts are
available, which caps any evening-before model. The temporal model is sensitive to the
seed (one seed stops after one epoch), so a deployed version should average several seeds.
The hybrids and the embedding ablations are negative results under this data and should
not be generalised.

\section{Conclusion}
Under the Phase~1 protocol, a compact PyTorch network with category embeddings and a GRU
over 14 days of delay history raises December PR-AUC from \XgboostTestPR{} to
\TemporalTestPR{} (seed 42; \TemporalSeedMean{} $\pm$ \TemporalSeedStd{} over three
seeds), with a day-level bootstrap interval above zero. The best recall reachable at 50\%
precision on the December curve rises from \XgboostRecallPfifty{} to
\TemporalRecallPfifty; at the threshold fixed on validation, recall rises from
\XgboostRawRecall{} to \TemporalRawRecall{} with raw probabilities and from
\XgboostRollRecall{} to \TemporalRollRecall{} with rolling recalibration. The GRU recovers
most of the signal of the hand-made history features from raw data. Two hybrids with the
Phase~1 XGBoost were implemented and evaluated honestly; neither beats the network.
Calibrated probabilities under the seasonal shift require rolling recalibration for any
model.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} Notebooks 06--10 in the \texttt{notebooks} folder of the project
repository (\url{https://github.com/techatanu/Flight_Delay_Risk}; Phase~1 is in
\texttt{ML\_Phase-1}) build every number, table and figure in this report from the
Phase~1 outputs, using PyTorch
\cite{paszke2019pytorch} and scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011}.
